Content-security detectors are the LLM-aware core of TrustGuard. They score prompts
and model output for jailbreaks, toxicity, and off-topic content, and they screen
documents and URLs for **indirect prompt injection** and PII.

Create them in the console under **Detectors**, then attach them from a
[policy](/trustguard/concepts/policies) to decide what happens when they match.

| Console card | Sides | Protocols | Configuration tab shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prompt Guard** | Input, Output | All | Protection Sensitivity |
| **Toxicity Detection** | Input, Output | All | Protection Sensitivity |
| **Custom Moderation** (Beta) | Input, Output | All | Topics you define + Protection Sensitivity |
| **URL Analyzer** | Input | LLM, MCP | Domains + two linked detectors |
| **Document Analyzer** | Input | LLM | Two linked detectors |

All of them are **detection-only**. The action — **Monitor**, **Block** — and the
phase — **Input**, **Output** — are set on the
[policy](/trustguard/concepts/policies) rule that references the detector, not on
the detector itself.

## Protection Sensitivity

Most content-security detectors have exactly one setting, and it is the same
control everywhere: three preset cards. You pick one; there is no number to type.

| Preset | Pill | What it does | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Permissive** | Low noise | Flags only clear, high-confidence threats. Your users rarely hit false blocks. | Internal tools and admin panels, developer-facing APIs, a trusted user base |
| **Balanced** | Recommended | Good coverage without over-blocking. The right starting point for most apps. | Employee-facing chatbots, B2B SaaS products, general-purpose assistants |
| **Strict** | High coverage | Catches everything possible. Expect some legitimate requests to be flagged. | Customer-facing chatbots, finance and healthcare apps, high-compliance environments |

**Balanced** is applied by default, and it is the right answer far more often
than people expect.

### What actually changes

The preset moves one thing: **how sure the detector has to be before it says
something**. It does not change what the detector looks for, and it does not make
it smarter — Strict does not catch a different *class* of attack, it catches the
same class earlier and less certainly.

That is why the trade runs in both directions:

- **Permissive** only reports what it is confident about. You will miss borderline
  attempts, and almost never argue with a finding.
- **Strict** reports things it is unsure about. You will catch more real attempts,
  and you will also flag someone quoting an article about prompt injection, a
  developer testing your agent, or a support ticket that happens to contain the
  word "ignore".

### Choosing one

Start on **Balanced** and leave it there until **Activity** gives you a reason to
move. The reason should be evidence, not caution:

| What you see in Activity | What to do |
|---|---|
| Real attempts getting through | Move to **Strict** |
| Findings you keep dismissing | Move to **Permissive**, or narrow the policy with a gate |
| A handful of findings that all look right | Leave it |

Going straight to Strict "to be safe" is the most common mistake here, and it
backfires in a specific way: the findings are not wrong so much as numerous, the
team stops reading them, and the one that mattered scrolls past unnoticed. A
detector nobody trusts is worse than one tuned slightly loose.

The provider behind these detectors and its credentials are **not** part of this
form. They are configured once for the workspace.

## Create a detector

The same four steps for every detector on this page:

1. Open **Detectors** and click **New detector** — or open the **Catalog** tab and
   click **Add detector** on the card you want.
2. On **Basics**, give it a **Name**. Use one that says how it is tuned, not what it
   is: *Jailbreak — strict* beats *Prompt Guard 2*, because the type is already
   shown next to it.
3. On **Configuration**, set what that detector exposes (below).
4. Click **Create detector**, then add it to a
   [policy](/trustguard/concepts/policies) rule with an action.

A detector on its own inspects nothing. Until a policy references it and that
policy is attached to a collector, it is inert.

## Prompt Guard

### The risk

Someone talking the model out of its instructions. "Ignore your rules and…",
role-play framing that makes the rule-breaking hypothetical, instructions hidden
in another language or spelled out character by character to slip past a filter
looking for phrases.

What makes this worth a detector rather than a keyword list is that the
successful attempts rarely look like the famous ones. They are rephrased,
translated, or buried, and the wording that works changes faster than any list
you would maintain.

### What it looks for

Attempts to override the instructions the agent was given. Direct ones — *"ignore
everything above"* — but mostly not: the same intent framed as a story or a
hypothetical, written in another language, spelled out character by character,
encoded, or simply reworded until it no longer resembles anything a filter would
recognise.

It judges intent rather than matching phrases, which is the point. A keyword list
catches the attempt you have already seen; the wording that works changes faster
than you can maintain one.

### Where it looks

**Input:** the message, before it is sent to the model.
**Output:** the reply, after the model produces it and before it reaches the
person.

Those are two different questions: on the way in, *is someone
attacking my agent*; on the way back, *did my agent get talked into something*.
Both are worth running, and the Output side is the one people forget.

It does not read tool results. An instruction that arrives inside a search
result or a ticket is a different problem with a different detector —
[Indirect Prompt Injection](/trustguard/detectors/agent-mcp-security).

<Note>
**A long, innocent preamble does not hide an attack.** Long text is scanned in
overlapping passes rather than only at the start, so padding an instruction
behind a wall of harmless prose does not get it through. This is a common bypass
and it is worth knowing it is covered, because it means a long prompt is not
automatically a safer prompt.
</Note>

### Configure

**Protection Sensitivity**, and nothing else. Start on **Balanced**.

Move to **Strict** when you see attempts getting through in **Activity** — not
before. Strict on a public chatbot will also flag people quoting an article
about prompt injection, or testing your agent out of curiosity, and a stream of
those trains everyone to ignore the findings.

## Toxicity Detection

### The risk

Two different problems that happen to share a detector.

On the way **in**, abuse of your service: someone using your agent to generate
hateful or harmful material, or simply abusing it. That is a conduct question,
and the useful response is usually to record it against the consumer rather than
to block a single message.

On the way **out**, what your agent is willing to say. A model that produces
something hateful, graphic, or harmful in a customer-facing reply is a far more
expensive problem than the same text arriving as input, and it is the direction
people most often leave unguarded.

### What it looks for

Toxic language — abusive, hateful, degrading or otherwise harmful.

It answers **one question**: is this toxic. There is no breakdown by kind, so a
finding tells you that a message crossed the line, not which flavour of harm it
was. If you need to separate, say, hate speech from violent content, that is
[**Custom Moderation**](#custom-moderation), where you define those subjects
yourself, not this detector.

### Where it looks

**Input:** the message, before it reaches the model.
**Output:** the reply, before it reaches the person.

Reading the findings in **Activity** is therefore about *who* and *how often*
rather than *what kind* — the useful pattern is a single consumer producing them
repeatedly, or a rise after a prompt change.

### Configure

**Protection Sensitivity**, and nothing else.

Consider running it in both directions but with different policy actions:
**Monitor** on input, where you mostly want the record, and **Block** on output,
where the cost of one bad reply is real.

## Custom Moderation

**Beta.**

### The risk

A subject specific to your business — a
competitor's product line, an internal project name, a regulated matter
particular to your sector, a product you discontinued and do not want discussed.

It answers one question per subject: *is this the topic I described*. That makes
it scope control rather than safety — nothing here is an attack, it is your agent
working correctly on something you never signed up to answer for.

### What it looks for

Whether the text belongs to a subject **you** described — judged against the
words in your definition, one answer per topic you defined.

### Where it looks

**Input:** the message, before it reaches the model.
**Output:** the reply, before it reaches the person — worth enabling, because
scope is as easily broken by what your agent says as by what it is asked.

On the coding-agent and agent platform collectors it sees the user's latest
message only, so a subject that comes up purely inside tool activity is not what
this detector is for.

### Configure

Every topic here is yours — its name, and crucially its definition. One detector
holds **up to five** of them.

Five is a ceiling on quality, not a licence limit. Every topic on a detector is
scored in the same pass as the text itself, and they share one budget: the more
topic definitions you load, the less of the message the detector actually reads.
Past a handful it scores fragments. **If you need more subjects, add a second
detector** — two focused detectors cost less to evaluate than one overloaded one,
and each keeps the full message in view.

The five count **everything on the detector**, whether you picked it from the
list below or wrote it yourself.

You do not start from a blank page. Thirteen common subjects come ready as
**editable drafts** — pick the ones that matter to you:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Religion | Code Generation | Language Translation |
| Politics | Violence & Harm | Adult Content |
| Financial Advice | Medical Advice | Illegal Activities |
| Hate Speech | Misinformation | Personal Information |
| Academic Dishonesty | | |

Add the ones that apply to your agent and **Customize** them, or **Add new
topic** and write your own. Either way what gets saved is your version — a
preloaded subject you never edit is still yours to tighten later, and the wording
you leave in it is the wording that gets scored.

Each topic opens a form in three parts:

| Section | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Name** | **Topic name** — *Politics*, *Competitor pricing* |
| **Instructions** | **Definition** — describe what the topic covers |
| **Examples** | Phrases that belong to it, one per line |

<Warning>
**The definition is what gets scored.** Content is matched against the words you
write in **Definition** — that field is doing all the work. The **Examples**
field is accepted and stored, but the detector does not currently read it.

So spend your effort on the definition. *"Political content"* will behave far
worse than *"Discussion of elections, political parties, candidates, government
policy, or partisan opinion."* Write it as if you were explaining the boundary
to a new colleague, and put the edge cases in the definition rather than
trusting an example to carry them.
</Warning>

Then set [**Protection Sensitivity**](#protection-sensitivity). It applies to
every topic you defined rather than per topic, so a subject you want watched more
closely than the rest is better written as a tighter definition than chased with
the sensitivity control.

## URL Analyzer

### The risk

An instruction hidden in a page the agent is asked to read. The link looks
harmless — a documentation page, a ticket, a shared doc — and the payload is on
the other side of it. Nobody in the conversation typed anything suspicious, which
is exactly why a prompt-side detector never sees it.

The same fetch also picks up personal data: a page the agent pulls into its
context can carry someone's details into a model that was never meant to see
them.

### What it looks for

Two things, in the text of the page it fetched: instructions aimed at the agent,
and personal data. The URL itself is not what is being judged — a perfectly
ordinary link to a page someone edited is the whole problem.

### Where it looks

Links in the message, **Input side only**, at the moment the message is
evaluated — before the model sees it. The page is fetched right then and scored
as it reads *at that instant*, so a page that changes after you tested it is a
different page.

Two consequences worth planning around. Links inside an uploaded file are not
covered here — that is **Document Analyzer**. And fetching happens at evaluation
time, so a page that changes after you tested it is scored as it is *now*.

Fetches run under an SSRF guard: HTTPS only, with internal, private and
cloud-metadata addresses refused, so a link cannot be used to make TrustGuard
reach into your network.

### Configure

This detector does not carry its own sensitivity or entity list. Instead you point
it at two detectors you have already created:

| Field | What to select | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Protection Sensitivity** | An existing **Prompt Guard** detector | Scoring fetched pages for indirect prompt injection |
| **Data Categories** | An existing **Data Loss Prevention** detector | Which PII to look for in fetched pages |

If you have neither yet, the console offers **Create a Prompt Guard detector** and
**Create a Data Loss Prevention detector** inline.

<Warning>
Linked detectors are shared, not copied. Retuning the Prompt Guard detector you
selected here also changes how this analyzer scores — and changes every other
detector and policy pointing at it. The console says as much when you select one.
</Warning>

Optionally restrict **Domains** with an allow list, a block list, or both. An empty
allow list permits every domain that is not blocked.

## Document Analyzer

### The risk

An instruction hidden inside a file someone uploads. A CV with white text on a
white background telling the screening agent to recommend the candidate; an
invoice carrying a line aimed at the assistant rather than the reader.

**This is content risk, not malware scanning.** A file can be perfectly clean by
every antivirus measure and still carry a hostile sentence — that sentence is
what this detector is for. It does not tell you whether a file is safe to open.

### What it looks for

The same two things as **URL Analyzer**, in the text it pulls out of the file:
instructions aimed at the agent, and personal data. Not viruses, not file
structure — what the document *says*.

### Where it looks

Files attached to the message, **Input side only**, before the model reads them.
It extracts the text first — PDFs, Office documents, plain text, and images
through OCR — and screens what it extracts.

OCR is the part that matters: an instruction rendered as a picture, or inside a
scanned page, is invisible to anything reading the file as text. It is also why
the same attack in a screenshot is caught here and nowhere else.

### Configure

The same linked-detector model as URL Analyzer: select an existing **Prompt Guard**
detector for injection screening and an existing **Data Loss Prevention** detector
for PII. The warning above applies here too.

**There is no sensitivity setting of its own.** How strict this detector is comes
from the Prompt Guard detector you linked, so the way to tighten document
screening is to raise that detector's
[**Protection Sensitivity**](#protection-sensitivity) — which also tightens
everything else pointing at it.

Nothing else to set. OCR and injection screening are always on — there is no
switch to forget, and no file-size or language list to tune.

## When to use

- **Prompt Guard** is the baseline for any chat traffic. If you deploy one
  detector, deploy this one.
- **Toxicity Detection** on output when your agent talks to the public; on input
  when abuse of the service is the concern.
- **Custom Moderation** for scope control — "only answer about X" — with the
  subjects written for your business.
- **URL Analyzer** and **Document Analyzer** for RAG and agent flows that ingest
  links or files.
- Tool results are a different detector:
  [Indirect Prompt Injection](/trustguard/detectors/agent-mcp-security).

Start every one of them with the policy in **Observe**. Move to **Enforce** once
**Activity** shows you are catching what you expected and not much else.
