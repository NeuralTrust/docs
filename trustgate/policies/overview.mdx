---
title: "Policies"
description: "A policy is a type, a scope and a configuration. The scope has two sides — who the traffic comes from and what it touches — and getting that pair right is most of the work."
---

Registry decides what a gateway can reach. Applications and access decide who may
call it. A **policy** decides what happens to the calls that are already allowed:
budgets, rate limits, what may be said, which tools may run.

Every policy is the same three things.

## Type

The **type** is what the policy does — a rate limit, an LLM budget, a prompt
template, a guardrail. Types come from the **Library**, grouped
as Traffic Control, Quota, Routing, Prompt Management, Tool Governance and
Guardrails. The Library is the authoritative list of what this gateway can
enforce; it grows without the console changing, so browse it rather than trusting
any list written down elsewhere.

You configure a type into a named policy, and you can configure the same type
more than once. Two rate limits with different numbers for different applications
are two policies of one type, not a conflict.

Name them so the list stays readable a year from now: the type is already shown
in its own column, so the useful name is the part the type does not say —
`Token budget · prod`, not `Token budget`.

## Mode

The **mode** is how the policy reacts when it matches.

| Mode | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Enforce** | Block the traffic that violates the policy. |
| **Observe** | Log the violation and let the traffic through. The decision the policy would have taken is recorded on the event. |
| **Throttle** | Delay the traffic that violates the policy instead of blocking it. Only the Rate Limiter offers it. |

Observe is how you find out what a policy would do before it does it. A type
that supports only one mode shows no choice: Tool Injection and the Per-Tool
Rate Limiter always enforce.

## Scope

Scope has two sides, and they answer different questions:

| Side | Question |
|---|---|
| **Requests from** | All traffic, specific **groups**, or specific **applications**. |
| **Resources** | All resources, or selected MCP servers — and, within a server, selected tools. |

A policy with both sides open is **gateway-wide**; narrowing either side makes it
**targeted**. The two sides intersect: a policy scoped to one group *and* one
server applies only where both are true, which is how you write "the contractors
group may not call the deployment tools" without touching anything else.

A tool only exists inside the server that serves it, so a tool-level scope always
follows from choosing a server first.

**Coverage** in the list is the consequence of all this — all applications, or the
number it actually reaches. Read it as the sanity check on a scope you thought
was narrower or wider than it turned out to be.

## Configuration

The type's own settings, with defaults tuned for the common case. Durations are
written the short way — `1h`, `30m`, `60s`, `500ms`.

Where a type needs credentials of its own — a provider's moderation API, an AWS
guardrail — they are stored encrypted on the policy and never shown again, the
same as anywhere else in the product.

Some types depend on something outside the gateway. A policy that hands traffic
to Agent Runtime needs a runtime collector connected to this gateway; without one
it has nothing to analyse with, and the policy will say so rather than failing
quietly at request time.

## Saving is enforcement

There is no draft state and no staged rollout. Saving a policy changes behaviour
for everything in its scope immediately, and deleting one removes that
enforcement just as immediately — which is why deleting asks you to type the
word.

Two habits follow from that:

- Check **coverage** before saving a change to a gateway-wide policy. A number
  larger than you expected means the scope is not what you think.
- Use **pause** rather than delete when you are testing whether a policy is the
  cause of something. Pausing stops enforcement and keeps the configuration and
  its attachments; deleting loses both.

A policy in **error** is not enforcing. It is not failing open silently — the
status says so — but nothing about the traffic it was meant to cover is being
checked until it is fixed.

## Where to attach

A policy can be attached from itself, by naming applications in its scope, or
from an application, by adding policies to it. Both edit the same relationship.

Gateway-wide policies need no attaching and cannot be opted out of by an
application — that is the point of them.
